Establishing a private easement across a neighbour’s land

Русский · English · 中文 · العربية

A private easement allows limited use of another person's land when a property cannot be reasonably used without such access. It is not a mechanism for obtaining a shorter or merely more convenient route. The court assesses necessity, lawful alternatives, the precise strip of land to be used and the least restrictive solution for the burdened owner.

What a private easement means

Article 274 of the Russian Civil Code permits limited use of another person's land for access by foot or vehicle, the construction, reconstruction or operation of linear facilities, and other objectively necessary purposes. The burdened owner retains ownership, use and disposal rights, subject to the defined restriction. A claim should specify the location, width, area, purpose, permitted vehicles, opening hours, duration and maintenance duties. A vague right 'to drive through' may be impossible to register or enforce.

When a court may grant access

The claimant must establish the right to the property requiring access, a genuine need, and the absence of a legally available and practically suitable alternative. The route and terms must be sufficiently precise to be reflected in the Unified State Register of Real Estate (EGRN). Where several possible routes exist, the least burdensome viable option should be chosen. A public road or other access that is longer or less convenient does not automatically justify an easement across a neighbour's plot.

Why negotiations matter

Under Article 274(3), an easement is normally established by agreement. A court determines the dispute if agreement on the easement or its terms cannot be reached. Before suing, send the landowner a written proposal identifying the cadastral plots, mapped route, purpose, proposed term, conditions and payment. Keep proof of delivery and the reply, if any. This helps establish what the disagreement is actually about, rather than treating correspondence as an empty formality.

Mapping the route and obtaining expert evidence

A practical easement requires a determinate corridor with boundaries and an area that can be recorded in EGRN. A cadastral engineer's diagram with characteristic-point coordinates, route width and total area is often central. A land-surveying or combined court expert examination may be needed. Questions for the expert can cover lawful access routes, technical feasibility, necessary width, safety requirements, effects on buildings and fences, and which route most preserves the neighbouring owner's use.

What evidence to prepare

Collect current EGRN extracts for both plots and the relevant buildings; land-survey and cadastral plans, maps and a recent topographical survey; documents showing why access is necessary; photographs and video of entrances, fences, terrain and obstacles; a plan of each proposed corridor with coordinates and area; an expert opinion or an application for a court examination; the written proposal and correspondence with the neighbour; and a substantiated calculation of the easement fee. Check first whether a registry-boundary error, rather than an access problem, is the real dispute.

Payment for the burden

Unless otherwise provided by law, the owner of the burdened plot may demand proportionate compensation. This is not a purchase price. Important factors include the area and length of the restriction, how often and by which vehicles the corridor is used, the extent of interference, and reasonable maintenance costs. Payment may be one-off or periodic. The court can consider the parties' calculations, valuation reports and expert evidence. Neither a nominal amount nor an excessive sum should be presumed correct without analysis.

Duration and permitted use

An easement can be temporary or indefinite. Temporary access for construction or repair should correspond to the required works; sustained access to lawful property can justify a longer term. The order or agreement should address pedestrian access, permitted vehicles, time-of-day limits, opening gates, utilities maintenance, surface repairs and other necessary terms. An unnecessarily broad right to unlimited heavy-vehicle access could be refused or narrowed.

When the claim may fail

Typical problems include an already available and adequate lawful route, a request motivated only by convenience, an indeterminate corridor, substantial interference making normal use of the burdened land impossible, or lack of title to the property requiring access. The Supreme Court’s 26 April 2017 review explains that an easement is not established merely to provide access to an unauthorised building; its legal status must be resolved first. The claim must seek limited use, not permanent possession of the neighbour's land.

What to ask for in the claim

Describe the cadastral number, exact coordinates, strip area and width, purpose, access types, duration, hours, payment amount and method, and maintenance obligations. Attach a graphic plan matching the wording of the request. Identify the proper owners and check jurisdiction: rights to immovable property usually require close attention to territorial jurisdiction, and the court system can differ where the dispute is commercial. The operative wording must be usable without starting another dispute.

After the judgment and ending the easement

Once a judgment has entered into force and describes the necessary terms, the restriction is registered according to applicable law. Neither party may unilaterally enlarge the corridor or change its purpose. If the grounds cease—for example, a suitable independent route becomes available—the owner of the burdened land may seek termination under Article 276 of the Civil Code. Changes may be agreed or, where contested and legally justified, considered by a court.

Seven practical steps

Obtain up-to-date EGRN records; identify and test all lawful access alternatives; commission mapped options from a cadastral engineer; send a proposal with route, terms and payment; preserve the correspondence and proof of need; formulate specific claims and expert questions; and register and observe the final easement. The outcome depends on the evidence, not on the fact that the neighbour refuses entry.

Frequently asked questions

Is a shorter route enough? Generally no: necessity and absence of an adequate alternative must be established. Does the neighbour lose the land? No: ownership remains but use is limited within a registered strip. Is payment always relevant? The burdened owner generally may seek proportionate compensation. Can heavy trucks use the route? Only if such use is needed, proved and covered by the easement's terms.
