Russian Supreme Court: waterfront land and good-faith buyers

English analytical adaptation of a Russian case review, not a substitute for the Russian original or individual legal advice.

A registered purchase does not cure every land restriction

A buyer may acquire an individually registered plot and later face a claim that part of it overlaps the public shoreline strip of a water body. Registration in EGRN and good faith are important facts but do not by themselves legalise land that Russian law prohibits from privatisation.

The Supreme Court case

Item 1 of Review of Judicial Practice No. 1 (2026) concerns two plots intended for individual housing construction, purchased in 2013. After a 2022 investigation, the environmental prosecutor alleged that their boundaries included part of the shoreline strip of the Klyazma Reservoir. A geodetic overlay identified the intersection. The Supreme Court, in determination No. 4-KGPR25-48-K1, set aside the appellate and cassation decisions and restored the first-instance ruling.

Shoreline strip is not a water-protection zone

Article 6(6) of the Russian Water Code generally provides a 20-metre public shoreline strip for relevant public water bodies, subject to statutory qualifications. Article 27(8) of the Land Code restricts privatisation of land within that strip. A water-protection zone imposes activity restrictions but is not the same legal regime; neither is a coastal protective strip or a sanitary protection zone. The precise legal boundary and applicable rules matter.

Good faith and the land register

The Supreme Court's reasoning does not mean every plot near a reservoir belongs to the state. The claimant must prove that the specific land actually falls within a legally protected public shoreline strip. Good-faith purchase and registration cannot override a direct statutory privatisation prohibition once that factual and legal basis is established.

Limitation periods require factual analysis

In the reviewed dispute the prosecutor discovered the alleged violation in August 2022 and sued in December 2022. The Court considered whether the competent public authority knew or should have known earlier. It would be inaccurate to assert that every shoreline claim is automatically exempt from limitation periods or that the purchase date always starts the clock.

Evidence and technical examination

Useful materials include EGRN coordinate data, plot formation history, boundary plans, water register information, legally established shoreline data, geodetic overlays and documents on overlapping zones. Where technical boundaries are disputed, a reasoned request for land-surveying and hydrological expertise may be appropriate. The expert measures and explains technical facts; the court decides the legal title.

Procedural strategy

Identify the exact claim, the legal regime asserted and the area of alleged overlap. Compare official maps and coordinate systems, test the claimant's methodology and consider whether independent evidence or expert examination is necessary. A bare assertion that the buyer acted in good faith may leave the decisive factual issue unanswered.

Important qualification

This is a case-specific Supreme Court position, not a general ban on private ownership of land in every water-protection zone. The Russian original includes a sample expert-examination motion and fuller source discussion.
