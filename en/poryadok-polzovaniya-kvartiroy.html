Apartment co-ownership: how Russian courts determine the use of rooms

Editorial translation of an analysis of Russian law and publicly reported case law. This is not a representation that the site owner acted in the cited case and does not guarantee any result. The sample claim below must be adapted to the parties, premises and current procedural requirements.

A co-owner is entitled to use an apartment, but a fractional share does not automatically correspond to a particular room. If the co-owners cannot agree, a Russian court must assess whether it can establish a workable arrangement for living in the premises without changing property shares, blocking access to common spaces or treating those spaces as one person's exclusive property.

A share in ownership is not a private room

After an inheritance, divorce or purchase of an interest in a flat, one owner may demand a particular bedroom because they own half the property. The other may object that the room is already occupied, exceeds the claimant's proportion, obstructs access to the bathroom or cannot realistically be shared. Saying “I own one half” does not resolve any of those issues.

Under Article 247(1) of the Civil Code of the Russian Federation, co-owners are to agree on possession and use; failing agreement, the court determines the arrangement. Article 247(2) allows each owner to claim use of a part proportionate to their share, or, where that cannot be provided, to seek appropriate compensation from owners who possess and use the part attributable to the claimant. Compensation is not automatic whenever the area of the rooms differs from the fractions of title: its legal basis, facts and amount must be established.

Determining the use of a dwelling is not a physical partition, an allocation of a separate title to a room, termination of co-ownership or a change of registered fractions. A claim seeking physical admission to the apartment, or the removal of obstacles to use, is a separate remedy and requires its own evidence.

What the Russian Supreme Court actually explained

Paragraph 37 of Joint Resolution No. 6/8 of the Plenums of the Supreme Court of Russia and the former Supreme Commercial Court, dated 1 July 1996, explains that impossibility of physically dividing property does not itself preclude a claim to determine its use. Courts should consider the established pattern of occupation, each party's needs and the actual feasibility of joint use. Existing use can deviate from the mathematical size of shares, but any departure needs a defensible factual justification.

Supreme Court determination No. 5-KG23-56-K2 of 8 August 2023 concerned owners of rooms in a communal apartment and disputes over access to common facilities. The decision supports the possibility of judicial arrangements where agreement is lacking. It must not be misconstrued as an unconditional right of anyone holding a tiny fraction of an ordinary apartment to receive a separate habitable room. The particular housing regime and facts matter.

Will the proposed room arrangement actually work?

Start with the floor plan, not with arithmetic. Identify isolated rooms, doorways, circulation routes and facilities used by everybody. Establish any long-standing pattern of occupation and the reasons to keep or alter it. Registration at an address may be relevant evidence but does not, alone, prove physical residence or exclusive rights to a room.

Personal conflict is relevant insofar as it is properly evidenced and makes joint use materially impractical. An arrangement that requires somebody to pass through the other's designated room, or blocks the kitchen or bathroom, may be unenforceable even when the square metres seem proportional. The defendant can point to lack of practical feasibility, disruption of settled use or disproportionate burdens. The claimant should consider alternative allocations instead of presenting one inflexible demand. A one-room apartment with irreconcilable interests is especially difficult: a title share does not guarantee exclusive use of its only living room.

Jurisdiction, documents and procedural risks

A civil dispute between private individuals over the use of residential real estate is ordinarily filed in a district or city court where the apartment is situated, subject to Article 30 of the Civil Procedure Code of the Russian Federation and verification of the exact claims and parties. The full list of co-owners should first be established from the Unified State Register of Real Estate (EGRN), because their rights may be affected by the judgment.

Useful evidence includes an up-to-date EGRN extract, floor or technical plan with room areas, a proposed occupancy scheme, proof of existing use, messages showing unsuccessful attempts to agree and relevant witness evidence. If the feasibility of the plan is contested, frame specific technical questions for a specialist or court-appointed expert rather than asking vaguely to “inspect the apartment.” Relevant procedural principles include Articles 56 and 67 of the Civil Procedure Code.

Calculate any court fee under the version of Article 333.19 of the Russian Tax Code applicable at filing, including exemptions and the combination of remedies sought. Do not add a financial compensation demand without separately proving the legal ground, period, amount and another owner's actual use of the claimant's portion.

This example is for co-owners of an apartment with more than one residential room and a technically workable proposed allocation. It needs substantial revision for social tenancy, ownership partition, compensation, a single-room dwelling or a communal apartment with a special legal regime.

Claimant: [full name, date and place of birth, address, one identifier required by Article 131 of the Civil Procedure Code, telephone and email if available]
Defendant: [full name, address and other identifying data if known; state when unavailable as required by Article 131]

STATEMENT OF CLAIM — DETERMINATION OF THE ORDER OF USE OF AN APARTMENT

I own [fraction] of apartment [address and cadastral identifier], as evidenced by [EGRN extract and details]. The defendant owns [fraction], and other owners hold [fractions]. According to [technical plan], the flat contains [number] living rooms measuring [areas] square metres, a kitchen, corridor, bathroom and [other premises].

No agreement on use has been reached: [dates of proposals and responses, with evidence]. In practice the premises are used as follows: [specific facts and documents]. I propose that room [number, area and location on the attached plan] be designated for my use; room [number] for the defendant; and the kitchen, bathroom, corridor and other service areas remain in joint use with free access for all co-owners. This scheme is feasible because [doorways, the parties' needs and access arrangements], and it does not change registered ownership shares.

On the basis of Article 247 of the Civil Code, paragraph 37 of Joint Plenum Resolution No. 6/8 of 1 July 1996 and Articles 30, 56 and 131–132 of the Civil Procedure Code, I ASK THE COURT:

  1. Determine the order of use of apartment [address, cadastral identifier] by assigning the claimant residential room No. [as identified on the attached plan], with an area of [value] square metres.
  2. Assign the defendant residential room No. [room and area], if consistent with the parties and the proven feasible arrangement.
  3. Declare the kitchen, corridor, bathroom and [specified facilities] for the common use of all co-owners, preserving access in accordance with the judgment.
  4. If legally justified and properly documented, award recoverable litigation expenses in the applicable procedural manner.

Verify actual room numbers and sizes on the technical plan. Where someone obstructs entry, separately assess and plead admission or removal of obstacles. Where allocation of rooms is impossible, reconsider the remedy instead of demanding an unworkable arrangement.

Conclusion: the court governs use, not legal ownership

The strongest claim is a verifiable proposal showing how every co-owner can use the flat, rather than a demand for “my fraction in square metres.” The Plenum guidance and Supreme Court decisions do not remove the need for individual proof. Compensation, admission and termination of co-ownership should not be included simply because the relationship between owners has become difficult.
