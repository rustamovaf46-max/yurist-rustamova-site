How to remove a neighbour’s fence or other obstruction from your land in Russia

Русский · English · 中文 · العربية

If a neighbour puts a fence, metal container, garage or another object on your land, the first step is to identify exactly what right is being infringed. A removal claim is not the answer to every disagreement: the proper remedy may be a negatory claim, release of land from occupation, a boundary dispute or correction of erroneous data in the Russian real-estate register.

1. Verify the legal boundary

Obtain an up-to-date extract from the Unified State Register of Real Estate (EGRN) and the land survey plan. Compare the recorded boundary coordinates with the actual location of fences and structures. Long use by a neighbour does not itself prove title to the strip. A cadastral engineer may need to set out the boundary points on the ground. If the recorded coordinates are themselves disputed, consider whether an EGRN registry-error claim is required instead.

2. Select the correct legal remedy

Article 304 of the Russian Civil Code allows an owner to demand removal of infringements not involving dispossession; Article 305 extends certain protections to lawful possessors. Depending on the evidence, the claim may ask that an identified part of the plot be vacated, a particular fence section or structure dismantled or relocated, or another defined obstacle removed. The request must be technically enforceable. There is no universal rule requiring every neighbour's container to be moved exactly one metre from the boundary: distances depend on the status and use of the object and applicable fire, construction and other rules.

3. Documents and evidence

Prepare an EGRN extract showing title and boundaries, cadastral maps and survey plans, a geodetic survey locating the disputed objects, dated photos and video, written exchanges with the neighbour, inspection reports, and records explaining how the obstruction prevents access, maintenance, repairs or use of the disputed land. Expert opinions and relevant earlier court materials may also be useful. Each exhibit should support a stated fact, not merely fill a checklist.

4. When a court expert examination is needed

Where the parties dispute the boundary, the occupied area or the location and removability of objects, ask for a land-surveying expert examination. Possible questions: Where does the recorded EGRN boundary run? Which objects cross it, where and by how much? What precisely is the affected area? What technically feasible removal options exist? The court determines rights and legal classification; the expert establishes technical facts. If fire-safety separation is relevant, ask separate technical questions concerning the applicable standards.

How to challenge an expert report

Compare the stated coordinates with the EGRN, the original boundary documents, the coordinate reference system, survey method, allowable error and diagrams. A reported overlap of 0.4 square metres does not automatically make the obstruction harmless or justify demolition of an entire building. The nature of the interference, measurement reliability and proportionate technical remedies matter. Under Article 87 of the Russian Code of Civil Procedure, an incomplete or unclear report can justify an additional examination; doubts as to its correctness or contradictions between reports may justify a repeat examination. Explain the specific defect rather than simply disagreeing.

5. Compensation for use of the land

Apart from removal, a claimant may in suitable circumstances seek money for unjust enrichment under Articles 1102 and 1105 of the Russian Civil Code. This requires evidence of actual use of another's land, relevant dates, affected area and a reasoned monetary calculation. The mere presence of an object near a boundary does not automatically prove a debt. Monetary demands have their own limitation rules; by contrast, Article 208 addresses an ongoing negatory infringement without loss of possession.

6. Bringing the case

Identify the right holder, establish the recorded and physical boundaries, record the obstruction, and determine who created or maintains it. State the act the defendant should be ordered to perform, with cadastral details and, where required, a georeferenced plan. Check the competent court, venue, current court fee and proof of serving claim documents on the other parties. Distinguish this from a claim to correct EGRN records or recover possession of land.

What the Supreme Court requires

Paragraphs 45–46 of Joint Supreme Court and Supreme Arbitrazh Court Plenum Resolution No. 10/22 of 29 April 2010 explain that a negatory claim requires proof of a protected right or lawful possession and an actual violation without dispossession; a real threat may also be relevant. A building-code breach alone does not remove the need to show that the claimant's right is affected. Registry coordinates do not automatically prove physical encroachment. The court should evaluate actual evidence and the expert's technical findings rather than delegate the legal conclusion to the expert.

Sample statement of claim (civil proceedings)

To: [district court at the location of the land]. Claimant: [full name, address, date/place of birth and identifier as required]. Defendant: [available identifying details and address]. Property: [cadastral number, address]. Court fee: [calculate under the current version of Tax Code Article 333.19 or state an exemption]. CLAIM: I hold [right] to the plot [number], supported by [document]. Since [date] the defendant has placed [identified object] at [coordinates or location on plan], preventing [specific use]. The violation continues, as shown by [documents]. Under Civil Code Articles 12 and 304 (and, where appropriate, 305) and Civil Procedure Code Articles 30 and 131–132, I REQUEST: (1) order the defendant to remove the obstruction by [specific technically feasible act] within [reasonable period]; (2) award properly evidenced litigation costs. Attach title and cadastral records, maps, survey, dated photos, correspondence, proof of sending documents to the parties, court-fee payment or exemption evidence and representative's authority, if applicable. [Date, signature]. Tailor this model to the actual case.

Jurisdiction, time limits and defence arguments

Claims about rights to immovable property are generally subject to exclusive territorial jurisdiction under Article 30 of the Civil Procedure Code; commercial disputes require separate jurisdiction analysis. Under Article 208 of the Civil Code and paragraph 49 of Resolution No. 10/22, a continuing infringement without dispossession is not subject to the ordinary limitation period, but that does not automatically apply to a separate monetary demand. Defendants commonly dispute boundary coordinates, whether an obstruction exists, whether the object is actually on their plot or whether the proposed removal is disproportionate.

Related remedies

If the legal boundary in EGRN is inaccurate, evaluate a registry-error correction claim. If lawful passage across another's plot is indispensable, the relevant remedy may instead be a narrowly defined private easement. Avoid a demand to 'remove all violations' without identifying the object and the actions required: an unclear judgment can be difficult to enforce.
