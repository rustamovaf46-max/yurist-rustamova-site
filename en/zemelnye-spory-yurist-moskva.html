Land dispute lawyer in Moscow: boundaries, EGRN errors and access to land

This is the full English-language explanation of the service under Russian law. Proceedings, evidence and remedies are governed by Russian legislation; the Russian version remains the primary legal text.

How the correct claim is selected

Fatima Rustamova represents parties in land disputes in Moscow and the Moscow Region. A land conflict cannot be reduced to a survey drawing alone. The remedy depends on the EGRN record, title documents, permitted use, special-use restrictions, buildings on the parcel, historic possession and the conduct of neighbours or public authorities. A claim to determine a boundary, correct an EGRN error, remove an obstacle, establish an easement or challenge an authority's refusal has a different subject of proof.

Boundary disputes and EGRN registry errors

A boundary dispute requires comparison of current EGRN extracts, survey and boundary plans, historic title records, archival plans and actual land use. An inaccurate coordinate in the register is not automatically a registry error: the parties may in fact dispute ownership, the extent of title or the historic location of the boundary.

Article 61 of Federal Law No. 218-FZ regulates correction of technical and registry errors. The requested judgment should identify the exact defect and the data that must change. A cadastral engineer's opinion may help prepare the case, while a court-appointed land survey examination is often needed where measurements or alternative boundary variants are disputed.

Fence, structure or another obstacle on the parcel

If a neighbour's fence, building, materials or access arrangement interferes with use of the parcel, the owner may seek removal of the continuing violation even without loss of possession. The legal basis commonly includes Article 304 of the Civil Code and Article 60 of the Land Code. The relief must be technically clear enough to enforce: it should identify the obstacle, its location and the action required rather than demand abstract compliance with the law.

Easement and access across another parcel

Under Article 274 of the Civil Code, an easement may permit limited use of another parcel where access or another necessary use cannot reasonably be secured otherwise. The court examines necessity, possible alternative routes, the burden placed on the neighbouring landowner, the proposed location and conditions, and payment where applicable. Convenience alone does not establish a right to use another person's land.

A useful technical opinion should compare realistic routes, explain construction and safety constraints, and show why the proposed option is proportionate. The court, not the expert, decides whether the statutory conditions are met.

Special-use zones and construction restrictions

Utility protection zones, sanitary zones, water protection rules and aerodrome-related restrictions may affect use and development. Their legal effect must be checked against the EGRN, official territorial documents and the rules applicable to the particular zone. The presence of a restriction does not always prohibit every form of construction, while the absence of an EGRN notation does not by itself prove that no public-law restriction exists.

Before litigation, it is important to separate a dispute over the existence or boundaries of a zone from a dispute over a permit, a refusal or compensation for restrictions.

Refusal to grant or register land rights

When a public authority refuses to provide land, approve a preliminary location scheme, register a right or accept an application, the precise written decision and its legal basis must be reviewed. Relevant facts can include the applicant's title to a building, formation and cadastral status of the parcel, permitted use, tender requirements and third-party rights.

A refusal to accept documents is procedurally different from a substantive refusal to grant a right. The proper judicial procedure and filing deadline depend on the authority, the applicant's status and the nature of the decision. Delay can deprive the claimant of the most effective remedy.

Evidence to collect before filing

  1. Obtain current EGRN extracts for the disputed and adjacent parcels and preserve the cadastral numbers.
  2. Collect title documents, survey plans, boundary coordination records, archival plans and correspondence with neighbours or authorities.
  3. Record the actual situation with dated photographs, measurements and, where appropriate, an inspection act or specialist opinion.
  4. Formulate the legal defect and the requested remedy before instructing an expert; evidence should answer the court's legal questions, not replace them.

Evidence gathered after a fence is moved or construction changes may no longer reliably show the original violation. At the same time, covert or unlawful collection of personal information is not required and can create a separate legal risk.

Land expertise: useful questions and risks

The court decides whether to appoint an expert, selects the institution, approves the questions and determines the advance payment. Typical questions concern the actual coordinates and area, overlap with registered boundaries, compliance with title documents, alternative boundary variants, the location of a structure and technically feasible access routes.

An expert should not be asked to decide who owns the land or whether a refusal is lawful. Those are legal conclusions for the court. A party may challenge the methodology and source data, question the expert, request clarification, and seek an additional or repeat examination where the procedural grounds are met. Expert expenses can ultimately be allocated as court costs.

Documents for the initial assessment

  • cadastral numbers and recent EGRN extracts;
  • sale, gift, inheritance or other title documents;
  • survey and boundary plans, coordination acts and cadastral engineer materials;
  • written refusals, orders, notices and correspondence with authorities or neighbours;
  • court documents and the next procedural deadline, if proceedings have started;
  • photographs or diagrams that do not disclose unrelated personal data.

For the first message, a short chronology and the cadastral number are usually enough. Do not send passports, signatures, medical records or confidential client files through open channels.

Stages of court representation

  1. Assessment: identify the remedy, parties, jurisdiction, limitation issues and any mandatory pre-trial procedure.
  2. Preparation: obtain records, formulate the claim or objections, calculate the state fee and prepare expert questions.
  3. Trial: file motions, examine documents and expert evidence, respond to counterarguments and preserve objections in the record.
  4. Review: analyse the judgment and the applicable appeal or cassation deadline. A new appeal is not a complete retrial in every case.
  5. Enforcement: ensure that the operative part can be implemented and that required EGRN or physical changes are properly documented.

Frequently asked questions

Can a case be filed if the boundary is not registered?

Possibly, but first the title documents and the reason for the missing coordinates must be examined. A cadastral procedure may be sufficient in some cases; in others a genuine dispute about rights or location requires a judgment.

Is a pre-trial letter always mandatory?

No single rule applies to every land dispute. A written proposal can clarify positions and support a settlement, but the mandatory procedure depends on the claim, parties and procedural code. It should not be allowed to consume a filing deadline.

Who pays for the court expert?

The court generally requires an advance from the party requesting the examination or otherwise determines payment during the case. Final allocation depends on the judgment and the rules on recoverable court costs. Read more about recovery of litigation expenses.

Does an expert opinion guarantee the result?

No. The court evaluates the opinion together with all other evidence. Incorrect source data, an incomplete question or a mismatch between technical findings and the requested legal remedy can weaken the case.
