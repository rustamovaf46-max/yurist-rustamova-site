Seven days before the hearing: Russian civil and commercial procedure reform

Fatima Rustamova — lawyer and court representative · 24.11.2026

As of 24 November 2026 this is a draft released for public consultation, not new rules in force. Its final wording may change.

The Ministry of Justice proposal published for consultation in November 2026 raises a question of genuine equality in Russian litigation. Procedural discipline is valuable only when it enables effective defence rather than becoming an independent barrier to access to courts.

Evidence disclosure: ending trial by surprise?

The draft proposes a seven-day pre-hearing disclosure period in civil and commercial court proceedings, allowing courts to set a different period for complex or voluminous cases. Disclosure is already required by Article 56(3) of the Civil Procedure Code (CPC) and Article 65(3) of the Commercial Procedure Code (APC). Supreme Court Plenum Resolution No. 15 of 21 May 2026 also explains advance disclosure in civil cases. The main change is a specific deadline. If a party receives hundreds of pages at a hearing, formally admitting them does not guarantee adversarial proceedings: time is needed to verify authenticity, compare records and prepare objections.

When late disclosure matters on appeal

Articles 46 and 123 of the Russian Constitution, CPC Articles 12 and 35, and APC Articles 8 and 9 protect a meaningful opportunity to present a case. Late disclosure does not automatically invalidate a judgment. An appeal should identify the late evidence, explain why it could not be examined, specify the applications made and demonstrate how the defect affected or could have affected the outcome. Record receipt dates, request time to review the documents and ensure objections are recorded in the hearing minutes.

Security for defendants’ costs: limits of proportionality

The draft introduces a mechanism for securing potential costs of defendants in Russian state commercial courts, not private arbitration. Published accounts of the draft indicate that a court could order financial security on application where cost recovery is at risk; failure to comply could leave the claim unexamined. This is not an automatic obligation for every claimant. Small businesses may be short of funds precisely because of the defendant’s conduct. Individual risk assessment, a proportionate amount and less burdensome alternatives are essential. Otherwise, protection against unrecovered costs may conflict with constitutional access to justice under Article 46.

In-house lawyers: proving case-specific expenditure

The Ministry proposes recognising representative expenses arising under employment as well as civil contracts, provided the expenditure is documented and connected to the case. This departs from the traditional treatment of ordinary in-house salaries as not themselves recoverable litigation expenses (Information Letter No. 121 of the former Supreme Commercial Court Presidium, 5 December 2007). If adopted, organisations will need to substantiate the case-specific share: assignments, time records, payroll calculations, payment evidence and separation from ordinary work. Double counting should be avoided and reasonableness assessed. An employment contract alone does not establish recoverable costs.

What litigants can do now

Send evidence to opponents early, retain proof of dispatch and receipt, and keep an evidence list. If new material appears at a hearing, request time to review it in writing and explain the steps needed to respond. Organisations can track staff work on litigation separately. Do not apply the proposed rules as current law until enactment, official publication and commencement have been verified.

Author’s conclusion

Justice depends on a party’s real ability to defend its rights, not the number of formal obligations. The proposal may reduce procedural surprise and improve costs recovery, but only if proportionality and access to courts are preserved. Security must not become a means of pressuring financially weaker litigants.
